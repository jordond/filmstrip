Overlays are effects, so they go in an `effects { }` block. They run after geometry has settled, which is what makes
their measurements fractions of the final frame. An overlay on a clip is measured against the clip's frame, and one on
the composition against the output frame. A clip with non-square pixels, like anamorphic footage, is measured at the
shape it shows at, so a square picture stays square on it.

## Image

`imageOverlay` draws a picture into a corner.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="image" />

Give it `at` instead of a corner and the picture's centre lands on that point of the frame. The point isn't held inside
the frame, so a picture placed near an edge hangs off it and the part past the edge isn't drawn.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="at" />

- `scale` is the picture's width as a fraction of the frame's width, a fifth by default. The height follows the picture's
  own aspect.
- `margin` is the inset from the corner as a fraction of the frame's shorter side, 4% by default. Both live on the
  overlay's `position`, an `OverlayPosition.Corner` or an `OverlayPosition.At`.
- `opacity` runs from `0f` to `1f`.

The image comes from an `ImageSource`, which is a path, a URI or encoded bytes. The picture is drawn in the color its
file describes, and an untagged picture is read as sRGB. SDR output clips a tagged picture's colors to BT.709, and an
HDR grade keeps them inside BT.2020. When a platform's decoder can't land the picture that way, the effect resolves as
degraded or unsupported and says why.

An export reads a path or URI picture as it is on disk when the export starts, so a file changed after planning draws
as changed and a deleted one is refused by name. A browser fetches a URI again for each export, and refuses it by name
if the fetch takes longer than 60 seconds. Bytes are held by value and never change.

## Text

`textOverlay` burns text into the frame. A preview and its export break lines on the same words.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="text" />

Every size in a `TextStyle` is a fraction of the frame. `fontSize` is the cap height against the frame's height, 6% by
default, and `maxWidth` is where lines wrap, 90% of the width by default. Colors are packed ARGB, as `0xAARRGGBB`, and
`backgroundColor` draws a plate behind the text.

Text has no margin of its own. The `anchor` names the same point on the text and on the frame, so `Anchor.BottomCenter`,
the default, puts the bottom edge of the text on the bottom edge of the frame.

## When it shows

Without a window an overlay draws for its whole scope. Both calls take `at`, a range of composition time where the end is
exclusive, or a `visibleDuring` `TimeRange` if you already have one.

## Animation

Every overlay takes an `animation`, asked once per output frame how to draw. `fade` ramps the opacity.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="fade" />

`fade` takes either side alone, and a side you leave out has no ramp. So `fade(inDuration = 500.milliseconds)` only
fades in. Each ramp is linear, and a range shorter than the two ramps takes the lower of the two opacities.

<Aside type="caution">
A clip's `fadeIn` and `fadeOut` ramp its audio, not its picture, and they aren't available inside `effects { }`. To fade
an overlay, pass `fade` as its `animation`. See [Audio](/guides/audio/#fades). A tape look that eases to its intensity
from another strength is its own `settle`, see [Filters](/guides/filters/#vhs).
</Aside>

`slideIn` and `slideOut` move the overlay between its authored position and an offset. `+` runs two animations together.
Opacities and scales multiply, and offsets add.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="slide" />

An offset is a fraction of the frame, with positive `x` towards the end edge and positive `y` down. An offset can carry
the overlay off the frame, and the part that leaves isn't drawn.

Anything else is a lambda, since `OverlayAnimation` is a `fun interface`. It reads an `OverlayTime`, where `elapsed` and
`remaining` count across the range the overlay is drawn over and `composition` is the frame's place on the timeline. It
answers an `OverlayFrame` with an opacity, an offset and a scale.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="custom-animation" />

The scale grows the overlay away from the point it's anchored by. On text it resamples the raster, and never lays the
glyphs out again.

A range with no end reads `remaining` as infinite, so the out side of a `fade` holds full opacity and `slideOut` holds the
authored position.

Animations are compared, since two overlays that draw differently can't share a cached frame. The built-in helpers
compare by value. A lambda compares by identity, so hold one instance and reuse it, or every rebuilt composition misses
the thumbnail cache.

An overlay at zero opacity, empty text or a window outside its scope draws nothing. The plan leaves it out and lists it
on its [inert notices](/guides/export/#inert-notices).

## Sprite sheets

A `SpriteSheet` turns one picture into a flipbook. Give `imageOverlay` a picture laid out as a grid, and the overlay
draws one cell of it at a time.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="sheet" />

The grid is uniform. Every cell is the same size, and the frames read left to right, then top to bottom. The grid
divides the picture as it's shown, after any orientation its file carries. A grid that doesn't divide the picture evenly
is refused by name, and never rounded. There is no padding, trim, rotation or atlas file.

- `scale` and the aspect belong to one cell, not to the whole sheet. A 4 by 3 sheet of square cells draws as a square.
- The frame count is the number of durations. When the last row isn't full, give `frameCount` or a shorter list, and the
  empty cells never show.
- `opacity`, `corner` and `margin` work as they do on a still.

Each frame lasts its own duration, taken as written, so a frame of 5 milliseconds is kept as 5 milliseconds. A frame
starts on the nearest output frame. A frame shorter than one output frame can land on the same one as the next frame and
never show, so pick durations that are at least one output frame long.

`plays` says how many times the frames run before the last one holds. It's `FramePlays.Forever` by default, and
`FramePlays.Once` plays through one time.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="sheetOnce" />

After a finite `plays` the last frame stays on screen for as long as the overlay does. End it sooner with `at` or
`visibleDuring`. That window also sets when frame 0 starts, so a sheet drawn from 2 seconds opens on its first cell
at 2 seconds.

Frames that don't last the same time take a list, one duration for each.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="sheetDurations" />

The short form, `SpriteSheet(columns, rows, frameDuration, frameCount, plays)`, builds the same sheet as a list that
holds `frameDuration` `frameCount` times over.

A sheet combines with an `animation`. They answer different questions, since the animation moves and fades the
placement and the sheet picks the cell.

## Animated pictures

A GIF, an animated WebP or an APNG given to `imageOverlay` plays its own frames. There is nothing to set.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/guides/Overlays.kt" region="sticker" />

The file says how long each frame lasts and how many times the picture plays. `scale`, `corner`, `margin` and `opacity`
work as they do on a still, and `scale` is the width of the canvas the file draws on, after any orientation the file carries.
Animated frames are turned the way a still is, on every backend. There is no `plays` to pass, so
the file's own count is the one in force. A file that plays forever loops for as long as the overlay does.

After a finite count the last frame stays on screen for as long as the overlay does, the way a browser holds it. End it
sooner with `at` or `visibleDuring`. That window also sets when frame 0 starts, so a sticker drawn from 2 seconds opens
on its first frame at 2 seconds.

An animated picture takes no `sheet`. A sheet and an animated file are two ways to read one picture, so giving both is
refused by name. An animated AVIF is refused by name too, and so is a file whose frames can't be read to the end.

Delays follow what a browser shows, with one difference. A GIF's delay of 10 milliseconds or less plays as 100
milliseconds. An APNG or a WebP plays its delays as stored, and only a stored zero reads as 100 milliseconds. A file
with frames of 1 to 10 milliseconds therefore plays faster here than in a browser tab, so if one looks too quick, check
its delays.

Every frame is decoded and held once the overlay resolves, so scrubbing backwards costs nothing. What a backend holds
for each frame differs, and a 16-bit APNG holds more of it than an 8-bit file. That count, with the canvases the decoder
works on, is held against the picture memory ceiling, and a file that goes past it is refused by name, before it is
decoded where the container allows. The same picture on two overlays is held once for an export or a preview.

## Composables

`filmstrip-compose` bakes any composable into an image overlay, so a title card, a sticker or a caption with a custom
font is written in Compose and drawn into the export. `filmstrip-compose-ui` puts the same element on a `VideoStage`,
where a drag moves it, a pinch scales it and a twist turns it.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/compose/ComposedOverlays.kt" region="title" />

An `OverlayTransform` says where the element's centre sits as a fraction of the frame, how much bigger it is drawn and
how far it's turned clockwise in degrees. An `OverlayCanvas` is the width the frame reads as to the content, 360 dp by
default, and as tall as the output's aspect makes it, so content written in dp lays out the same on a phone, a desktop
window and the export.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/compose/ComposedOverlays.kt" region="live" />

`ComposedOverlay` is controlled, so it moves only when the transform you pass back changes. `output` is the frame the
element is baked onto, which is `PreviewInfo.outputSize` or the export plan's output size, and only its proportions are
read on the stage. A drag holds the element's centre on the frame and a pinch holds the scale between
`ComposedOverlayDefaults.MIN_SCALE` and `MAX_SCALE`. Only a touch on the turned element is taken, so several stacked on
one stage each get their own, and `onSelect` hears which one was touched, so the sample keeps the index of the
selected title. Pass `enabled = false` to show an element without taking touches.

Mount one `OverlayCaptureHost` for the capturer somewhere on screen, then capture each element when you export.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/compose/ComposedOverlays.kt" region="capture" />

The capture lays the content out on the canvas, draws it the way the stage does and hands back an `ImageOverlay` at
`OverlayPosition.At` whose picture is exactly the pixels of the frame the element covers. It's null when none of the
element lands on the frame. Pass the composable that shows the element, never the text field it's edited with, or the
cursor is baked too. Content that loads a font or an image asynchronously holds `ready` until it has. Capture once,
when you export, and keep the baked overlays out of the preview's composition, so dragging never re-plans the preview.

The frame to capture on is the one the export writes, so plan once to read its size, capture every element on it and
plan again with the baked overlays added. The first plan only gives the frame, so its verdict is the second one's to
report.

<Snippet file="commonMain/kotlin/dev/jordond/filmstrip/docs/compose/ComposedOverlays.kt" region="export" />

<Aside type="note" title="Limits">
- A browser page can't read a file path, so an `ImageSource.Path` overlay is refused by name there. Pass bytes or a URI.
- A path or URI picture's encoded bytes must fit in 1 GiB. Past that it's refused by name.
- On desktop, an AVIF or HEIC image overlay (`iovl`) draws images that are none of them another grid or overlay. Its
  canvas plus every image it draws, each at its whole size, must fit in 1 GiB at 8 bytes a pixel, and a grid's picture
  plus its tiles the same. That's the only bound, however many images it holds. Past it the picture is refused by
  name. So is a grid or overlay alpha that doesn't read.
- An image overlay's canvas color is sRGB, and it's painted in the picture's own color. On a picture narrower than sRGB
  a color it can't hold stops at the nearest one it can. An overlay alpha's canvas reads as the luma its own coding
  gives that color, and an overlay alpha whose canvas shows under a matrix filmstrip can't read is refused by name.
- media3 uploads each overlay as one texture, so a picture or text longer on a side than the device's
  `GL_MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE` is refused by name. The browser does the same with WebGL's `MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE`.
- A sprite sheet's decoded picture counts against the picture memory ceiling on every backend.
- An animated picture's frames count against the picture memory ceiling on every backend, as a sprite sheet does.
- Every backend needs a known output frame rate to draw an animated picture, and refuses it by name without one.
- ffmpeg also needs the range an animated picture is drawn over to end, whatever the file's play count. It draws every
  frame as one atlas, so the atlas must fit both the picture memory ceiling and the frame size limit a sprite sheet
  has, about 16000 pixels a side for a square one.
- Android uploads one frame at a time, so the canvas the file draws on must fit the device's `GL_MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE`.
- The browser needs all the frames as one atlas that fits WebGL's `MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE`, and refuses a longer picture by
  name with the size in force. It also draws a 16-bit APNG at 8 bits.
- ffmpeg needs a known output frame rate and a drawn range with an end to draw a sprite sheet. It also can't open a
  frame past its own size limit, which for a square sheet is about 16000 pixels a side, and refuses a larger sheet by
  name.
- The browser needs the whole sprite sheet to fit WebGL's `MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE`. Android uploads one cell at a time, so it
  needs the side of one cell to fit the device's `GL_MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE`.
- media3 holds the point an overlay anchors to inside the frame, so one animated off the frame, or an `at` point past an
  edge, stops at the edge there. An `at` point inside the frame hangs off it the same way on every backend.
- On an 8-bit export ffmpeg draws an overlay in 4:2:0, so an odd `x` or `y` lands on the even pixel below it, one pixel
  up and left of the other backends. That holds for an animated overlay's position on every frame. See
  [Limits](/reference/limits/#placement).
- On an HLG grade, media3 blends an overlay in scene light, so the plan reports it as an approximated effect.
- The browser breaks lines at spaces and at each `\n`, and splits a word wider than a whole line. Android and Apple follow
  the platform's line breaking and the JVM uses its `LineBreakMeasurer`, so a caption can wrap on different words between
  them.
- The ffmpeg backend draws text with the fonts the JVM finds on the machine, and refuses text holding a character those
  fonts can't draw.
- A composed overlay is captured on the frame you pass as `output`. When the export frame differs from it, because the
  encoder aligns the size or `heightPx` caps it, the backend resamples the picture.
- A composed overlay is an 8-bit sRGB picture. On an HDR grade its white lands at the 203 nit reference white like any
  overlay picture.
- A composed overlay's rotation is fixed when it's baked. Its `animation` moves and fades the baked picture.
- Colour emoji in a composed overlay need a font that has them, and a browser needs one bundled with the app.
- Apple holds 20 bytes for each pixel of a composed overlay's picture, which is at most one output frame.
- Android 8 and 8.1 capture through a different snapshot path than Android 9 and later, and only Android 16 was
  measured.
- Composed overlays go on the composition's output frame only, since a clip's frame size isn't public.
</Aside>
